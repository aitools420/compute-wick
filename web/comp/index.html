Where that leaves us.

Strengths

  • Agents are the first customer, not a feature. Native MCP over the same core as the UI, listed in the official registry. Only GPUsMarket shares the thesis, and they sell one market — we aggregate.
  • Zero fee we can actually afford. One process on commodity hosting; fee_bps: 0 published in every response. A $20M burn rate cannot copy this price.
  • BYO-key brokering. We place rentals with your provider key and never store it. Every funded competitor wants to be your wallet instead.
  • The tape. Price history per model and class recorded every half hour since first boot — a past no late entrant can backfill. Only SkyPilot keeps anything comparable.
  • The tripwire → limit orders. Under-the-market alerts with no account — and since 2026-08-24 they execute: standing limit orders via signed fill tickets and the open-source keyholder sidecar, custody-free. Nobody else has either half, let alone the pipe.
  • Idle capacity as the editorial spine — interruptible-first ranking, the idle index, the discount instrument. The cheap half of the market is our home turf, not an afterthought.

Weaknesses

  • Ten feeds vs Shadeform's thirty. Coverage is our thinnest wall; it grows a signup at a time.
  • The tape is young. Days of history where Compass shows quarters. Time fixes this and only time.
  • No unified billing. A team that wants one invoice across clouds goes to Shadeform or Prime Intellect today. That is Stage 3, unbuilt.
  • No hardware, no SLA, no enterprise story. We are an instrument over other people's machines and say so.
  • No brand, no track record. Unnamed product, zero testimonials, a book that has never had a paying renter through it.
  • No hyperscaler quotes — deliberate, but it means the enterprise comparison shopper bounces.

Points of difference

  • Instrument, not reseller. Everyone funded wants to sit between you and the provider and clip the flow. We read the market, point, and step aside — the renter pays the provider's own price.
  • Machine-first distribution. Registry listing, llms.txt, keyless REST — discovered by agents, not ad spend.
  • Honesty as interface. Stale flags shown, unrated models named, "not a forecast" printed on the timing instrument, fee stated in-band. In a market full of "from $0.99*" tables this reads different because it is.
  • A product with a world. The pond, the frog, the field-journal voice — nobody mistakes this for another dashboard template, and nobody forgets it either.
  • The five-minute money book. The feeds people actually rent from refresh every five minutes; the sleepy catalogs every thirty — and we print which is which. Sheets refresh weekly and hide it.
  • Every price carries its own tape. Each row in the market table draws that model's last 24 hours inline, off history we recorded ourselves. A table a competitor can copy in a day; the tape behind it they cannot.
  • Accountless to the bone. The book, the API, the MCP endpoint and the price alerts all work with no signup, no email, no cookie wall. Metering is an optional bearer token. Everyone else gates at the inbox.
  • The workload lens. The price column itself answers "for MY work": flip the market table to training and it re-prices every card in FP16 TFLOPS-hours per dollar; flip it to inference and it prices the VRAM that actually gates serving; flip it to bandwidth and it prices the ceiling for memory-bound work. Spec-sheet ceilings, never benchmarks, unrated cards named and sunk — the honesty is what makes the comparison usable, and nobody else re-prices a live book this way.
  • Safety rails built for unattended buyers. Rentals dry-run by default, price caps absolute, an optional budget guard that destroys the rental at the cap — its own failure mode disclosed in the receipt — and idempotent fills that cannot double-rent on a retry. Exchange-grade paranoia, documented.
